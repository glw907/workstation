# Making a mail message list fast to scan: unread state and sender (evidence notes)

Research date: 2026-10-13. Provenance tags used on each claim: **[PR]** peer-reviewed paper or its abstract; **[REV]** peer-reviewed review or theory article; **[PRAC]** practitioner guidance (NN/g, essays); **[DOC]** official product or standards documentation; **[SRC]** product source code read directly; **[OBS]** third-party review or user report of a shipping product; **[2nd]** second-hand or snippet-level (I saw a search-result summary or a citing paper, not the primary text). Older-than-2020 observations are dated inline.

Headline: there is strong vision-science evidence that a unique, high-contrast feature (hue, size, luminance contrast) among homogeneous items is found quickly, and that too many competing emphasised items destroys that benefit. I found no peer-reviewed study that tests unread styling, sender styling, or bold versus dot versus colour bar in a mail list. Everything about those specific choices is inference from the general findings plus observed product practice.

## 1. Pre-attentive pop-out, and bold versus colour versus a small marker

### Takeaway
Colour, size, motion and orientation are the "undoubted" guiding attributes in visual search; luminance polarity is "probable"; font weight (bold) is not on Wolfe and Horowitz's list at all and I found no direct study of bold as a search cue in lists. A unique feature pops out only if it differs a lot from its neighbours AND the neighbours are homogeneous, so the strength of any unread cue depends as much on how many other things are emphasised as on the cue itself.

### Cited Findings
- Feature Integration Theory (Treisman and Gelade): features such as colour or orientation are registered in parallel in separate feature maps, so a target with a unique feature is found regardless of the number of distractors, while a conjunction of features needs focused, serial attention. Full citation: Treisman, A. M., and Gelade, G. (1980). A feature-integration theory of attention. *Cognitive Psychology*, 12(1), 97-136. [REV/PR] [2nd: described via the Healey and Enns survey; I did not retrieve the original abstract] — [Healey and Enns 2012, section 3.1](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf); [bibliographic record](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(80)90005-5)
- Healey and Enns define a task as preattentive when it can be done on a large multi-element display in under about 200-250 ms; since a saccade takes at least 200 ms to initiate, the viewer completes it in one glance. Their example: a red circle among blue circles pops out "regardless of the number of distractors", whereas a conjunction target (red circle among red squares and blue circles) does not. Healey, C. G., and Enns, J. T. (2012). Attention and visual memory in visualization and computer graphics. *IEEE Trans. Visualization and Computer Graphics*, 18(7), 1170-1188. [REV] — [Healey and Enns 2012](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Treisman later softened the strict dichotomy: parallel and serial are ends of a spectrum, and "the amount of difference between the target and the distractors will affect search time" (a long vertical line among short ones is found immediately, a medium one more slowly). [REV] — [Healey and Enns 2012, section 3.1](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Duncan and Humphreys' similarity theory (as summarised by Healey and Enns): search efficiency falls as target-nontarget similarity rises, and falls as similarity among the nontargets drops (a heterogeneous background). [REV] — [Healey and Enns 2012, section 3.3](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Wolfe's Guided Search 6.0 restates this: guidance to a target is stronger when target-distractor (TD) differences are larger and when differences among distractors (DD) are smaller. Wolfe, J. M. (2021). Guided Search 6.0: An updated model of visual search. *Psychonomic Bulletin and Review*, 28, 1060-1092. doi:10.3758/s13423-020-01859-9. [REV] — [GS6 full text (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965574); [author PDF](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/Wolfe2021_GS6.pdf)
- GS6 also says: guidance needs a much larger feature difference than mere detectability (about 10-15 degrees for orientation, "similar effects occur in color", citing Nagy and Sanchez 1990); guidance works on coarse, categorical representations (a target in a unique category is easier to find); and rules that hold for one guiding feature "do not necessarily apply to another". [REV] — [GS6 author PDF, "Five forms of guidance" and "Preattentive guidance is complex"](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/Wolfe2021_GS6.pdf)
- GS6: Palmer et al. (2019) showed that even basic colour guidance takes about 200-300 ms to become fully effective, and basic feature searches do not have perfectly flat slopes (RT rises with the log of set size). A salient item that is not the target can still capture attention bottom-up: in GS6's Fig. 4 the blue items attract attention even though the target is orange. [REV] — [GS6 author PDF, "Preattentive processing takes time"](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/Wolfe2021_GS6.pdf)
- GS6's abstract: guidance "will not be uniform across the visual field. It will favor items near the point of fixation." [REV] — [GS6 abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965574)
- Wolfe and Horowitz's Table 1 grades candidate guiding attributes. Undoubted: colour, motion, orientation, size (including length and spatial frequency). Probable: luminance onset (flicker), luminance polarity, shape, curvature, line termination, closure, stereoscopic and pictorial depth. Possible: lighting direction, glossiness, expansion, number, aspect ratio. Doubtful: novelty, letter identity and alphanumeric category. "Probable non-attributes" include colour change, intersection, optic flow, faces, your own name, semantic category. Font weight or stroke thickness is not listed anywhere. Wolfe, J. M., and Horowitz, T. S. (2004). What attributes guide the deployment of visual attention and how do they do it? *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 5, 495-501. [REV] — [Wolfe and Horowitz 2004 PDF, Table 1](https://search.bwh.harvard.edu/new/pubs/WolfeHorowitzNRN04.pdf)
- Luminance difference works like hue difference: in visual search, search time rose linearly with the number of distractors when the target-distractor luminance difference was small but was "roughly constant" when it was large, and "results were similar for targets defined by luminance and chromaticity"; combining luminance with chromaticity helped only when the target was dimmer than the distractors. Nagy, A. L., and Sanchez, R. R. (1992). Chromaticity and luminance as coding dimensions in visual search. *Human Factors*, 34(5), 601-614. [PR] — [abstract via Europe PMC record, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872089203400507)
- Typographic highlighting in running text: Strobelt et al. tested nine common techniques crowd-sourced; "increasing font size works best as a single highlighting technique" and "there are significant visual interferences between some pairs of highlighting techniques". Strobelt, H., Oelke, D., Kwon, B. C., Schreck, T., and Pfister, H. (2016). Guidelines for effective usage of text highlighting techniques. *IEEE TVCG*, 22(1), 489-498. doi:10.1109/TVCG.2015.2467759. [PR] — [Harvard VCG page with abstract](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20160101-guidelines-for-effective-usage-of-text-highlighting-techniques)
- A course slide deck summarising that paper (a student presentation, so weaker than the paper) reports Study 1: 673 words with 20 highlighted, find as many as possible in a time limit, 45 MTurk participants; strong pop-out for font size, border and colour (background colour possibly beating text colour "because coloured area is larger"); weak for letter spacing and italics; recommended low-interference pairs include "bold + yellow background". The deck does not state bold's own rank. [2nd] — [UBC CPSC 547 slides on Strobelt et al.](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/courses/547-15/slides/jordon-texthilight.pdf)
- Bold and word recognition (not search): boldface words were recognised faster in lexical decision, but only for low-frequency words (28 participants, per a secondary summary); the abstract cites Pelli et al. (2006) for bold letters being recognised more efficiently under visual noise. Macaya, M., and Perea, M. (2014). Does bold emphasis facilitate the process of visual-word recognition? *Spanish Journal of Psychology*, 17, E2. doi:10.1017/sjp.2014.2. [PR] — [abstract via Europe PMC record, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/sjp.2014.2)
- NN/g's eyetracking taxonomy names a "spotted pattern": users fixate words that stand out, "such as links, colored or bold text, and bullets", or that match their task. [PRAC] — [NN/g, Text Scanning Patterns: Eyetracking Evidence](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)
- Practitioner caution on bold: bold works by drawing the eye during skimming, even when the reader is not reading, but its effect is "inversely proportional to how often it's used"; Fowler cites no studies and rests on observation. Fowler, M. (2026-01-28). Excessive Bold. [PRAC, opinion] — [martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ExcessiveBold.html)
- Failure mode of a weight-only cue in a shipping product: 2025 Samsung community posts report unread Gmail rows on One UI 7 phones as "less bold" and harder to tell from read ones (dark mode: white text for new, gray for read helped). Low-quality forum evidence. [OBS, 2025] — [Samsung Community thread](https://eu.community.samsung.com/t5/galaxy-s24-series/new-gmails-no-longer-in-bold-ui-7/td-p/12368286)

### Inferences
- A small coloured dot or bar in its own column is the textbook "unique feature among homogeneous distractors" (colour is an undoubted guiding attribute; absence of the marker on read rows makes the field homogeneous). It should therefore be found with high efficiency, provided no other coloured items compete in the same region.
- Bold is a weaker, less well-characterised cue than hue or size: it has no row in Table 1, it changes stroke width (spatial-frequency/size and local ink density) rather than a single dimension, and the 400-vs-700 step is a small difference relative to the "much greater than discrimination" threshold GS6 describes for guidance. It is still a legitimate "spotted-pattern" cue (NN/g) and is robust when the whole row of text is bold, because the bold block is large. No source compares bold, dot and bar head to head, so ranking them is a design judgement, not a finding.
- Weight steps that are small (for example 600 versus 700) risk falling below the guidance threshold; Thunderbird's own card CSS sets read senders at weight 600 and unread at bold (see section 5), which is an instance of that risk.
- Because Nagy and Sanchez found luminance contrast as effective as hue contrast, an unread cue need not rely on hue: a high-luminance-contrast mark (dark dot on a light row) should pop out as well as a coloured one, which also helps with WCAG 1.4.1 (section 6).
- GS6's foveal bias suggests the marker should sit close to where the eye already rests during scanning (next to the sender name), not at a far edge of a wide table.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests font weight as a visual-search cue in lists or tables; the Strobelt paper is the closest, but I could not retrieve its full text (IEEE, closed access, no repository copy found), so bold's rank and interference numbers are unknown.
- Healey and Enns' Fig. 2 (the feature list with references) is an image; I could not read it. Wolfe and Horowitz Table 1 was used instead.
- Treisman and Gelade (1980) original abstract not retrieved (Elsevier paywall); the FIT description is from Healey and Enns.
- No source quantifies how the pop-out of a dot changes with dot size or eccentricity in a list UI.

## 2. Colour coding: benefits, how many codes, redundancy, clutter cost

### Takeaway
The classic Human Factors literature says colour helps search and identification in some tasks and hurts in others; people can reliably use only a handful (about 5-10) of absolute colour codes, and more than about seven rapidly discriminable colours is not supported. Colour works best as a redundant code and when the colour is rare; used widely for low-priority information it creates the heterogeneity and clutter that remove pop-out.

### Cited Findings
- Christ's review covered 42 studies from 1952 to 1973 comparing colour codes with achromatic codes; its conclusion was conditional: colour "may be a very effective performance factor under some conditions" but can be detrimental under others. Christ, R. E. (1975). Review and analysis of color coding research for visual displays. *Human Factors*, 17(6), 542-570. doi:10.1177/001872087501700602. [PR] [2nd: from the SAGE abstract page as surfaced by search; the page returned HTTP 403 to me, so I did not read the full abstract or paper] — [SAGE abstract page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/001872087501700602)
- Later papers citing Christ are reported (in search-result summaries) to say: colour is the most effective cue for aiding visual search; colour coding can be distracting in a search task when the target's colour is not known in advance; and, in a separate reading of the colour-versus-achromatic literature, colour was not clearly better than achromatic displays on performance although users preferred it. [2nd: I did not open these papers and did not confirm which one contains which statement; treat as leads] — candidate sources: [Graphics Interface 1982 paper](https://graphicsinterface.org/wp-content/uploads/gi1982-37.pdf); [Learning-technology paper on a colour-coding reversal effect (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8576459/)
- How many colours: Healey ran a visual-search experiment on the number of colours viewers can distinguish simultaneously and found viewers "can rapidly choose between up to seven isoluminant colors". [REV, citing PR] — [Healey and Enns 2012, section 4](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Absolute identification is far lower than discrimination: a DTIC report states that people can discriminate very many colours in the lab but the number they can reliably identify in real settings "varies from three to ten"; Conover is cited at 5-8 and Bishop and Crook (1961) at about nine hues plus white with luminance and purity levels, for sets of about 30 combinations. [2nd, search-result snippet of a government report] — [DTIC ADA155013](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA155013.pdf)
- Redundancy: colour as a redundant code in a complex display, and colour coding giving fewer errors and faster responses than shape coding, is attributed to Smith in a Michigan State thesis. [2nd] — [MSU thesis](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/13796/OBJ/download)
- WCAG's Understanding document treats a lightness difference of at least 3:1 as a valid extra cue next to a colour difference, and lists pattern and text as redundant cues (G14, G111). [DOC] — [W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- Cost of competing cues: Healey and Enns report a feature hierarchy in which hue and luminance can mask weaker features. In Callaghan's boundary-detection work, random hue variation slowed detection of shape patterns while random shape variation did not affect colour patterns. In Healey and Enns' own study, "random variations in hue degraded performance for detecting size and density targets" while texture variation did not affect hue detection. Their design rule: "the most important data attributes should be displayed with the most salient visual features, to avoid situations where secondary data values mask the information the viewer wants to see." [REV/PR] — [Healey and Enns 2012, sections 3.7 and 5.1](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Clutter: "excess and/or disorganized display items can cause crowding, masking... greater difficulty at both segmenting a scene and performing visual search"; their Feature Congestion measure rests on the idea that the more cluttered a display, the harder it is to add a new item that would reliably draw attention, and measures correlated well with search time in complex imagery. Rosenholtz, R., Li, Y., and Nakano, L. (2007). Measuring visual clutter. *Journal of Vision*, 7(2), 17. doi:10.1167/7.2.17. [PR] — [abstract via Europe PMC record, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/7.2.17)
- NN/g: "If everything is contrasted, then nothing stands out"; "In common, uncomplicated designs, limit your color use to 2 primary and 2 secondary colors"; "Do not rely only on color to communicate visual hierarchy." Gordon, K. (2021-01-17). Visual hierarchy in UX: definition. [PRAC] — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/)
- Highlighting techniques interfere with each other when stacked (Strobelt et al., abstract). [PR] — [Harvard VCG](https://vcg.seas.harvard.edu/publications/20160101-guidelines-for-effective-usage-of-text-highlighting-techniques)

### Inferences
- If tags, flags, junk, attachment and star icons are all coloured, the list's distractor set becomes heterogeneous (high DD) and a coloured unread dot is no longer a unique feature. Reserve hue for the one or two states the user ranks highest (unread, then perhaps flagged), and render the rest in neutral grey-scale or low-saturation form.
- Tag colours chosen by the user are an unavoidable source of heterogeneity; keep the unread cue on a channel that tag colour cannot occupy (a leading dot or bar in its own column, plus weight), which also follows the feature-hierarchy rule above.
- Staying within the "about 5-7 reliably usable" range is relevant if tag colours are meant to be identified by colour alone; beyond that, tags need a text label.
- Use colour redundantly with a second channel (presence of a marker, weight, position), which is both what the colour-coding literature favours and what WCAG 1.4.1 requires.

### Gaps
- I could not read Christ (1975) or the later Human Factors colour-coding papers in full (SAGE blocks automated access); the "42 studies" and "may be detrimental" statements rest on the abstract as surfaced through search, and the citing-paper claims are second-hand.
- No source found on colour use for low-priority metadata in list UIs specifically; the "cost" evidence is general (feature hierarchy, clutter, NN/g).
- The 5-10 absolute-identification range comes from a snippet of an old government report, not from a primary paper I read.

## 3. How people scan lists and tables; where the sender sits

### Takeaway
Eyetracking shows reading is strongly left-biased and front-loaded: people attend to the first two words of a line, spend about 80% of web viewing time on the left half, and fixate items that stand out (the "spotted" pattern). This favours putting the sender (and the unread cue) at the start of each row, but the evidence is for web pages and newsletters, not for mail lists specifically.

### Cited Findings
- F-pattern: Nielsen's eyetracking of 232 users on thousands of pages found a rough F shape (a top horizontal scan, a shorter second one, a vertical sweep down the left); readers notice the first two words of a line far more than the third; the shape varies (E, inverted L) with layout. Published 2006-04-16; an 11-year follow-up reportedly confirmed it. [PRAC, large-sample industry eyetracking, not peer-reviewed] — [NN/g, F-Shaped Pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/)
- NN/g scanning patterns: F-pattern ("in the absence of subheadings and bullets, users tend to fixate on the words toward the beginning of lines"), spotted pattern (fixations on words that stand out or match the task, such as bold or coloured text, digits, capitals), layer-cake (fixations on headings, called the most effective way to scan), commitment (reading most content words, when motivated). [PRAC] — [NN/g, Text Scanning Patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)
- Left bias: "Web users spend 80% of their time viewing the left half of the page and 20% viewing the right half" (up from 69% in 2010); more than 120 participants, about 130,000 fixations; search result pages 94% on the left; the leftmost zone is typically navigation. Fessenden, T. (2017-10-22). [PRAC] — [NN/g, Horizontal Attention Leans Left](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-attention-leans-left/)
- Inbox-level evidence: NN/g's eyetracking of 42 participants across 117 newsletters found users "extremely fast at both processing their inboxes and reading newsletters"; fully reading only 19% of newsletters; the "first two words" heatmap caption is about headlines inside the newsletter, not inbox rows. Nielsen, J. (2006-06-11). [PRAC, dated] — [NN/g, Email Newsletters: Surviving Inbox Congestion](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/email-newsletters-inbox-congestion/)
- Sender weight: "People simply don't open messages that don't have recognizable sender information"; many inbox views truncate From fields after 20 or 25 characters, so the first characters carry the load. Nielsen, J. (2008-10-19), on transactional email. [PRAC, dated] — [NN/g, Transactional Email and Confirmation Messages](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/transactional-and-confirmation-email/)
- Subject lines: users "often read only the first few words of a subject line"; inbox views limit visible characters; scanning of "sender information and subject lines" decides survival in a crowded inbox. Estes, J. (2014-05-04). [PRAC, dated] — [NN/g, Email Subject Lines: 5 Tips](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/email-subject-lines/)
- A Mimestream reviewer's rationale for bolding senders: "the most important fact about a message is likely who sent it to you." Engst, A. (2023-05-24). [OBS, opinion] — [TidBITS](https://tidbits.com/2023/05/24/why-i-use-mimestream-for-gmail/)
- Adjacent HCI studies exist on inbox triage but concern what content cues drive prioritisation, not styling: Cox et al. ran a field experiment (45 participants, 360 emails each over three weeks) on four inbox-level cues (urgency, time needed, importance, importance salience). Cox, A. L., Bird, J., Brumby, D. P., Cecchinato, M. E., and Gould, S. J. J. (2021). Prioritizing unread e-mails: people send urgent responses before important or short ones. *Human-Computer Interaction*, 36(5-6), 511-534. doi:10.1080/07370024.2020.1835481. [PR, abstract level via search summary] — [Birmingham repository record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/prioritizing-unread-emails(50f03819-7631-4047-b19e-24d576b0d157)/export.html)
- Wainer, J., Dabbish, L., and Kraut, R. (2011). Should I open this email? Inbox-level cues, curiosity and attention to email. *Proc. CHI 2011*, 3439-3448. doi:10.1145/1978942.1979456. [PR; I confirmed the citation but did not read the abstract] — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1978942.1979456)

### Inferences
- The sender is the first piece of text on the line in a card layout (Thunderbird and Apple both put it first) and sits in a leading column in a table: that matches the F-pattern and left-bias evidence, so sender styling and the unread cue should live at the left edge of the row, not the right.
- Date and counts belong on the far right: that is where attention is lowest, so low emphasis costs little there.
- Truncation (20-25 characters of From, about 40 of subject in many clients) means sender prominence should rest on the first words, not on a long, uniformly styled string; give the sender column enough width that the display name is not cut before it is recognisable.
- Because users read subjects mainly by their first few words (NN/g), subject styling can stay quiet when the sender is strongly styled: the eye has usually committed to the row by then.
- All of the eyetracking is from web pages and newsletters, 2006-2017; transfer to a dense mail list is plausible, not demonstrated.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed eyetracking study of a message list (card or table) located; no study of whether sender or subject gets more fixation time in a list.
- Wainer et al. (2011) and Miller and Charles (2017; cited by an Expert Systems paper as finding sender and subject are the main deciding factors) were not read; do not rely on them for numbers. Marketing-blog statistics on sender-name open rates are unsourced and excluded.
- No data comparing card (two-line) versus table (one-row) scanning speed.

## 4. Visual hierarchy in dense lists: number of levels, de-emphasis, over-emphasis

### Takeaway
Practitioner guidance converges on three levels or fewer of contrast, secondary information shown smaller or less saturated, and emphasis kept scarce. The vision-science backing is the feature-hierarchy and distractor-heterogeneity results: emphasising everything removes the contrast that makes any one thing findable.

### Cited Findings
- Limits on levels (NN/g): "Use no more than 3 contrast variations for complex designs" and "If everything is contrasted, then nothing stands out"; "Use no more than 3 sizes"; limit big elements to a maximum of 2. Gordon (2021). [PRAC] — [NN/g, Visual Hierarchy in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/)
- De-emphasis (NN/g): "less-saturated colors can be used for items of lesser importance"; "Make less-important elements smaller"; containers such as borders and backgrounds "can create visual clutter, so use them sparingly"; the hierarchy comes from "contrast in value and saturation between the element and the context", not from the colour itself. [PRAC] — [NN/g, Visual Hierarchy in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/)
- NN/g on data tables: freeze header rows and columns, keep the first column as an identifier, order columns by importance, and use borders, zebra striping or hover highlighting to help track a record; it does not address de-emphasising secondary data. Laubheimer, P. (2022-04-03). [PRAC] — [NN/g, Data Tables: Four Major User Tasks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Feature-hierarchy rule: salient features for the data users most need; secondary values must not mask them (Healey and Enns, quoted in section 2). [REV] — [Healey and Enns 2012](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Over-emphasis: bold effectiveness is "inversely proportional to how often it's used" (Fowler 2026, opinion). [PRAC] — [martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ExcessiveBold.html)
- Observed de-emphasis of secondary metadata in shipping products: Mimestream shows the message count "in a much lighter color" because it is "not as significant" (TidBITS, 2023) [OBS]; Thunderbird's card CSS sets the date at opacity 0.85 and the per-card action button at opacity 0.5 (section 5) [SRC].

### Inferences
- A workable budget for a list row: level 1 = the unread cue plus sender; level 2 = subject (normal, full contrast); level 3 = date, counts, preview, icons (reduced weight or contrast). That is three levels, which is within NN/g's limit, and puts the strongest feature on the information the user ranked highest (unread, then sender).
- An all-bold unread row (sender and subject both bold, as Gmail, Outlook and Thunderbird cards do) spends the emphasis budget on two fields; a design that must fit more emphasis elsewhere (tags, flags) could bold only the sender and keep the subject regular.
- Reducing date contrast is supported by NN/g and by observed practice, but the reduction is bounded by the text contrast minimums (WCAG 1.4.3, covered by another researcher); Thunderbird's 0.85 opacity is mild.
- Confidence note: the "three levels" numbers are a practitioner heuristic, not an experimental result.

### Gaps
- Material Design 3 and Apple HIG list guidance would be the natural primary design-system sources; the Material 3 page is JavaScript-rendered and returned no body to my fetcher, and I did not retrieve Apple HIG list or typography pages.
- No controlled study of how many emphasis levels a dense list tolerates.

## 5. How mature mail clients show unread state and sender (as of 2026-10-13)

### Takeaway
No client I checked relies on a colour change alone for unread: each uses a leading marker (dot or bar), bold text, or both. Sender or subject is bold in most (Gmail, Outlook, Thunderbird cards, Mimestream sender); Apple Mail's official docs describe a blue dot, with bold only as a column-layout option. Documentation of exact styling is thin for Gmail and Fastmail.

### Cited Findings

#### Apple Mail (macOS)
- Official: "Email messages you haven't read yet have a blue dot or star (for messages from VIPs) next to them." [DOC] — [Apple, Read and respond to emails in Mail on Mac](https://support.apple.com/en-au/guide/mail/mlhlp1010)
- Official Viewing setting: "Display unread messages with bold font": "In column layout, show unread messages in bold to help distinguish them from messages you have read." Also a "List Preview" setting for the number of message lines shown in the list. [DOC] — [Apple, Change Viewing settings in Mail on Mac](https://support.apple.com/guide/mail/cpmlprefview)
- iCloud.com Mail's guide lists a blue circle as the unread icon (per search summary). [DOC, 2nd] — [Apple, Read email in Mail on iCloud.com](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/guide/icloud/mm6b1a0b80/icloud)

#### Gmail (web)
- No Google help page I found describes the unread styling; the "Mark messages as read or unread" page covers actions only. [DOC] — [Gmail Help: mark messages as read or unread](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/12516?hl=en-GB&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop)

## 6. WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1 Use of Color and unread indicators

### Takeaway
SC 1.4.1 (Level A) forbids colour as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response or distinguishing a visual element. An unread indicator that is only a colour change (for example blue versus black text at the same weight) fails unless the colour pair also differs in lightness by at least 3:1; a dot present/absent, bold, or a bar is a non-colour cue.

### Cited Findings
- Normative text: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element." Level A. [DOC] — [W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- Intent: colour may be used, but must be complemented by another visual indication; a colour difference that also has a lightness difference counts as an extra cue if the contrast ratio between the colours is 3:1 or greater; when users must accurately perceive or tell apart a particular colour, an additional indicator is still required. [DOC] — [W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- The document's examples: required fields marked with red text and an icon; errors "shown in red" need a non-colour cue; links must be visually evident without colour (failure F73); identifying required or error fields by colour alone is failure F81. Techniques: G14 (also available in text), G111 (colour and pattern), G183 (3:1 contrast to distinguish inline links), G205. [DOC] — [W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- The criterion also treats colour that signals an action or a change of state (for example an entry updated) as within scope. [DOC] — [W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- Text colours must still meet the contrast minimums of SC 1.4.3 (noted in the same document's link discussion). [DOC] — [W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)

### Inferences
- Unread cues that pass on their face: a bold sender or subject, a leading dot or bar (its presence or absence is a shape difference), a row-background tint plus one of those. Cues that fail alone: recolouring unread text or dates (Mimestream's blue unread date, Thunderbird's `--unread-color` text colour) with no weight change, unless the two colours differ in lightness by 3:1 or more.
- Colour of the dot itself is decoration if the dot's presence carries the meaning; the dot's own contrast against the row background is a separate matter (SC 1.4.11, which I did not retrieve; the other researcher covers contrast).
- Programmatic exposure of unread state to assistive technology (a separate matter from 1.4.1, probably under 1.3.1 and 4.1.2) is not covered by the retrieved source.

- No official WCAG example specifically about message lists or read/unread state exists in the retrieved page; the application above is my reasoning.
